## The problem

Real estate and construction run on WhatsApp threads and phone calls. That holds fine for two people. Put a sales team, an office and a site crew on the same project and it stops holding.

Messages get buried. Nobody's certain who approved what. A property gets marked sold in one thread and the person who needed to know finds out days later.

There's also nowhere central to look. Customer records sit in old chats. The sales pipeline lives in one person's head, which makes that person the bottleneck for every question about it. The office stays a step behind whatever is happening on site.

Talking it through, it came apart into five problems that fed each other:

- WhatsApp has no structure to hold any of this
- Site and office work off different information
- Nothing is searchable once the thread scrolls past
- Customer records are scattered across people's phones
- Nobody can see the sales pipeline

## What research changed

I started with my uncle, who has spent twenty years in construction and real estate. Growing up around the business gave me the vocabulary, not the problem. In-person interviews gave me the problem, and I kept them running the whole way through instead of treating research as a phase that closes.

Three findings did more to shape the product than the feature list did.

Everyone wanted fewer calls, not more software. Nobody asked for a platform. They just wanted to stop repeating themselves.

Site workers had no experience with enterprise software of any kind. Whatever I drew had to be legible to someone whose entire reference for an app was WhatsApp.

And nobody wanted all of it. Each role wanted the two or three things their job actually needed, and treated the rest as clutter.

## The decisions

### Five features, shown as channels

Not modules, not a dashboard with a nav rail. Channels, because that's the shape WhatsApp had already taught this audience. Announcement, Tasks, Material, Inquiry-record and Booking, under a heading that just says Channels. So a new user never had to learn an information architecture, just five names.

### Three roles rather than one app

Since nobody wanted all five, the product ships as three views. Admin, office and site each see the channels their job needs, with permissions cut the same way.

A site worker opens Sitenote to the channels their job needs and never sees the rest. I think that's most of the difference between software people use and software people are told to use.

### Material Design 3, because WhatsApp got there first

Borrowing a language they already read fluently meant onboarding could be about the work instead of the interface. It cost nothing to decide and saved a lot of explaining later.

### Booking, where the money actually moves

Research made it obvious that booking and sales had the most to gain from being centralized. Project details, property listings and customer records sit in one place with the account history attached, and every listing carries its own message thread, so the conversation about a property lives on the property instead of in a group chat.

The pipeline turned into something you could look at instead of something you had to ask someone about.

### Inquiry records, so a lead isn't one person's contact list

A buyer rings someone, or messages them, or turns up at the site office. Whoever takes it owns that lead from then on and it lives in their phone. If they're away, or they leave, the lead goes with them. That is the scattered customer records problem at its most expensive, because a lead is the start of everything the booking channel later records.

So every inquiry became a record of its own, with its own thread, in the shared workspace instead of a private chat. Anyone whose role allows it can see where a lead got to and pick it up mid-conversation.

This is where the thread turned out to land hardest, though I only found that out later. Five in six inquiries grew a conversation, against under a third of listings. That reads about right: a live buyer is something people need to talk about, and a filed unit usually isn't. Inquiry records ended up the second most used channel in the product, ahead of the ones I had assumed would carry more.

### Activity, so nobody has to keep checking

You can't open an app every five minutes to see whether a teammate marked a property sold. WhatsApp had trained everyone to do exactly that.

Activity isn't one of the channels. It sits in the bottom bar beside Home and Team, because it isn't a place work lives, it's a place you check. Updates come to you there, scoped to the projects and listings you're actually on, so staying current stopped being a job in itself.

### Tasks, so work stops living in someone's head

Admins create and assign day-to-day work to office and site users, with progress tracked on the task itself instead of reconstructed from a call.

That was the intent, and it isn't what happened. Fifty-six tasks in two years. Most of them were people assigning work to themselves, only two ever reached a site user, and half never got closed at all. The ones that did took a median of about three months. The channel I drew as a delegation tool turned into a private to-do list, and mostly went unused.

## Where it landed

Sitenote went live on the web in April 2024 and ran for twenty-three months without a gap at the company it was built for. Sangani in construction and Opera in real estate work out of the same workspace, and fourteen people were still active in it in February 2026.

That March it thinned out to one user. Two more companies had been set up on Sitenote by then, and they carry most of the current work, so the product has outlasted the client it was made for. All three workspaces were active in August 2026.

## What I'd do differently

Building this as the founder and its first user taught me more than a brief could have. The daily design demos and weekly stakeholder meetings did the most for quality. They kept me close to what clients needed and to the engineering constraints sitting behind every decision.

The mistake was spread, and pulling the numbers two years on put a figure on it. Booking took 83% of everything anyone did in the product and the inquiry records took most of what was left, so two of the five channels carried 98% of the work. Tasks, material and announcements split the other 2% between them. Material was used by one person and then by nobody.

Booking was the feature the business actually ran on, and I let the secondary channels pull focus before it was locked. Given it again, I'd ship booking on its own and get it into daily use before touching anything else.
